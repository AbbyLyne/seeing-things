Double Take: guess first

Before you scroll: a driver starts on pole. Where do they usually finish?

And if you scored how closely the finishing order follows the starting order, from 0 (no link at all) to 1 (identical every time), what score would you give it?

Write your guess down, then open the box.

TipTake a second look

Of the 85 drivers who started on pole and finished the race, 60% won, and 75% finished first or second.

And how closely does the finishing order follow the starting order overall? On a scale from 0 (no link) to 1 (everyone finishes exactly where they started), it scores 0.73: a strong link, but far from a certainty. I guessed 0.37.

The picture

Take a second look: Look at the right-hand side of the chart: drivers who start 15th or further back usually finish a few places higher than they started. Part of that gain is free: when cars ahead retire, everyone behind them moves up a place.

Some of it is also fast cars that started out of place, after a grid penalty or a bad qualifying, working their way back to where they belong.

WITH finishers AS (

    SELECT
        result_id,     -- carrying through so the mart has a unique key to test
        season,
        round,
        driver_code,
        team,
        grid,
        finish_position
      FROM {{ ref('stg_f1__results')}}    -- ref() is what draws the lineage arrow
     WHERE grid > 0                       -- pit-lane starts have no grid slot to compare - removing
       AND finish_position IS NOT NULL    -- only classified finishers; this is exhibit logic
)

SELECT
    result_id,
    season,
    round,
    driver_code,
    team,
    grid,
    finish_position,
    -- positive = progressed forward, negative = lost places
    grid - finish_position AS places_gained,
    -- average gain per team in the season, repeated on all rows for that team and season
    avg(grid - finish_position) OVER (PARTITION BY season, team) AS team_avg_gained
  FROM finishers

What to notice: the bootstrap resamples whole races, not rows, because drivers in the same race affect each other; the second model adds each team’s car for each season, so it compares team-mates.

In plain English

Drivers who start near the front usually finish near the front. But fast cars qualify well and race well, so a lot of what looks like the power of pole position is really the power of the car.

The technique

Spearman’s rank correlation (family: association), then linear regression with a covariate (family: modelling).

Grid and finishing positions are both ordinal: the order matters, but the gap between 1st and 2nd isn’t necessarily the same as between 15th and 16th. Spearman works on ranks, so it doesn’t assume equal gaps. The regression then asks how much finishing position changes per grid place, first on its own and then comparing drivers in the same car (each team in each season counts as one car).

What the numbers say

  • Across 1,609 classified finishes in 92 races (2022–2025), the rank correlation between grid and finish is 0.73 (95% interval 0.69 to 0.77, resampling whole races). That’s a strong link.
  • Ignoring the car, each place further back on the grid goes with finishing about 0.65 places further back (95% CI 0.62 to 0.68). Grid alone explains 52% of the variation in finishing position.
  • Comparing drivers in the same car, that falls to about 0.42 places (95% CI 0.38 to 0.45): 36% smaller. Knowing the car raises the share explained to 64%.

The brain trap: narrative fallacy

We like tidy stories: “he won because he started on pole”. The start is visible and memorable; the car’s pace across the whole weekend isn’t, so the story gives the credit to the grid slot.

I fell for a version of it before running any code. I guessed the link was weak (0.37) and that the car explained little of it (8%). Both guesses were wrong in the same direction: I under-weighted the car.

Verdict: Confounded

Grid and finish are strongly linked, but car pace drives both: fast cars qualify near the front and race near the front. Compare drivers in the same car and grid’s effect shrinks by about 36%.

What’s left still isn’t proof that the grid slot itself causes better finishes. A driver who out-qualifies their team-mate may simply be quicker that weekend, and this model can’t tell the two apart.

What would change the verdict? A case where grid slots move for reasons unrelated to speed, such as grid penalties for engine changes. Comparing penalised drivers with their own unpenalised races would isolate the grid slot itself.
